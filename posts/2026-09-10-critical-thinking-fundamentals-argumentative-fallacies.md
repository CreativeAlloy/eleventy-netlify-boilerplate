## Argumentative Logical Fallacies

In this lecture, we are going to explore the massive library of logical errors known as Fallacies in critical thinking. More specifically, the types of fallacies we are about to cover are known as **Argumentative Logical Fallacies.**

Popular fallacies, such as the **Strawman Argument,** can be categorized as rhetorical instead of argumentative. The difference is that in a Strawman Argument, one party invents a position the other party has never claimed to defend - especially one that is effortless to reject - in order to "be correct" about something. This is what I call a **Rhetorical Logical Fallacy.** It may be logically valid in isolation, perhaps even sound, except no counterargument has been mentioned at any point.

An **Argumentative Logical Fallacy** is a type of logical inconsistency performed during the argumentation process itself, violating the soundness of the syllogism. The goal is not always merely rhetorical, and those fallacies strictly represent operational errors.

### Hasty Generalization

A **Hasty Generalization** is an argumentative fallacy representing any precipitated conclusion that has been reached based on minimal data.

Oftentimes, hasty generalizations are a type of **Inductive Reasoning.**

Unlike **Deductive Reasoning,** which we have explored many times during Critical Thinking Fundamentals, **Inductive Reasoning** is the act of invoking a general from the particular. In other words, if a private case has specific qualities, those qualities are extended to all other cases in the same category:
1. **Premise:** An Israeli person punched me in the face.
2. **Conclusion:** Therefore, all Israelis are mindlessly violent.

Slow down. An isolated incident - no matter how unpleasant - does not paint every single individual who belongs to the nation of Israel as "mindlessly violent." This is the **Fallacy of Hasty Generalization** in action.

Another example of Inductive Reasoning (and thus Hasty Generalization):
1. **Premise:** I have checked five classrooms, and all windows were closed inside.
2. **Conclusion:** All windows must be securely closed across the building.

Five classrooms with closed windows do not guarantee that all windows in the building are closed. Unless every particular case is verified, such a conclusion cannot be logically drawn.

Whether one, two, three hundred, or four thousand Israelis punch people in the face, and whether one or one million windows are closed in a building, **none of this guarantees the qualities of the remaining cases.** There exist 12 million Israelis who may or may not have punched people in the face.

### Argumentum ad Verecundiam (Appeal to False Authority)

The fallacy that describes a situation where any claim is accepted unconditionally because the person presenting their claim is a self-proclaimed field expert and/or a representative of a particular popular institution, is known as **Argumentum ad Verecundiam.**

Authority figures do not possess inherent omniscience that somehow makes them capable of declaring the truth at all times. On the contrary - having an institutional position is oftentimes the exact platform propagandists use in order to spread their agenda, trusting that their audience will accept their narrative without any objections:

3. **Conclusion:** Therefore, Tom Fletcher must be telling the ground truth, and those babies will starve ($Q$ is true).

### Argumentum ad Populum (Appeal to Popularity)

Related to Argumentum ad Verecundiam, Argumentum ad Populum represents the logical fallacy of accepting any claim unconditionally simply because many people agree with it.

The problem with accepting a narrative based on its popularity is that its integrity is never verified against reality. No matter how many people believe something to be true, it does not mean it is.

A very digestible historical example is that most people used to believe the Earth was flat. Except it was later discovered that all planets, Earth included, share the same spheroid shape (sometimes called planetoid). Suddenly, the most popular claim about our planet turned out to be a complete fabrication based on Christianity, proving that widespread support does not absolve anything from being false.

### Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent

In the following example, we will explore the Fallacy of Affirming the Consequent within the framework of Academic Consensus Bias:

> *"A man has fallen into the river in Gaza City $(Q)$. Hey! It's Israel's fault $(P)$! The IDF are committing genocide in Gaza, therefore it's those Israel Offense Forces that pushed him to drown in the river! I know this because the UN have confirmed time and time again that the IOF are committing genocide in Gaza against the Palestinians! Because if the UN ever claim anything $(U)$, it has to be true $(N)$!"*

$$ (U \rightarrow N) \therefore (Q \rightarrow P) $$

With text: **Because the UN says Israel is guilty (U), it must be the infallible truth (N).**
**Therefore $(\therefore)$, whenever any bad event occurs in Azzah (Q), it is guaranteed that Israel caused it (P).**

The formula above represents a compound of an Affirmed Consequent and the Academic Consensus Bias. For an Affirmed Consequent in isolation, the proper formula is as we have previously established:
1. **Major Premise:** If the IDF push a man into a river in Azzah $(I)$, he will drown $(Q)$.
2. **Minor Premise:** *"A man has fallen into the river in Gaza City! Hey!"* ($Q$ is true).
3. **Conclusion:** Therefore, the IDF pushed him into the river in Azzah $(I)$.

You cannot use the truthfulness of a particular consequent to establish with certainty that one specific antecedent is the cause. As established in [Conditional Reasoning:](https://thewaspalloy.org/posts/critical-thinking-fundamentals-conditional-reasoning/)

> *A Material Implication looks like this:*
> *1. **Major Premise:** If it's raining ($P$), the ground must be wet ($Q$).*
> *2. **Minor Premise:** It's raining ($P$ is true).*
> *3. **Conclusion:** The ground is wet ($Q$ is true).*
>
> *The reason this is important is that there is a crucial axiom to remember about Modus Ponens: **the falsity of a particular antecedent does not necessarily lead to the falsity of the consequent.** Attempting to confirm the falsity of $Q$ based on the falsity of $P$ alone is known as the **Fallacy of Denying the Antecedent.***
>
> *If the weather is sunny, it does not strictly mean the ground is not wet. A garden hose or a fire hydrant could easily make the ground wet. **Not-$P$ does not lead to Not-$Q$, because the truthfulness of $Q$ does not depend on the truthfulness of $P$.***

The same principle is true if the ground were wet (Affirming the Consequent). $Q \rightarrow P$ is logically unsound because nothing guarantees that the outcome was caused specifically by rain. It could have been the same fire hydrant or garden hose from the example.

Likewise, the man from our example may have fallen into a river in Azzah, except that does not guarantee an IDF soldier pushed him inside:
- A Hamas or PIJ terrorist could have pushed him.
- Another man might have pushed him due to personal conflict.
- He might have fallen inside due to intoxication.

And arguably the most important takeaway from this section: **this is the foundational mindset driving criminal investigations everywhere.** Finding a dead body somewhere says nothing about the culprit. Evidence must be gathered in order to discover whether a murder occurred to begin with, and understand the nature of the incident.

### Non-Sequitur

A **Non-Sequitur** is a logical fallacy that describes any conclusion that does not logically result from the established premises:
1. **Major Premise:** Criminals $(C)$ often carry guns $(G)$.
2. **Minor Premise:** An Israeli man was carrying a rifle $(G)$ on the street.
3. **Conclusion:** This Israeli man is a criminal $(C)$.

**There is no supporting evidence for this conclusion.** It is a Non-Sequitur precisely because the antecedent of carrying arms does **not** lead exclusively to criminal activity. Israel Defense Soldiers carry their weapons around during breaks, when off-duty for a few days, or when on duty directly within the boundaries of Israel:

![Israel Defense Soldiers Taking a Walk.](/static/img/IDF_Soldiers_Walking.jpg "Israel Defense Soldiers Taking a Walk.")
*Image Source: [Friends of the IDF](https://www.fidf.org/)*

As seen in the example above, these young Jewish men are taking a leisurely walk while having a conversation. They are carrying rifles, but they are also wearing standard IDF uniforms. Concluding that they must be criminals due to the presence of rifles around their shoulders ignores all other possibilities - in this case, the fact these are soldiers defending their own country from terrorism.

Another example of a Non-Sequitur is the following:
1. **Major Premise:** Criminals $(C)$ are known for cutting human flesh $(F)$.
2. **Minor Premise:** A person is cutting into the flesh of an unconscious human being $(F)$.
3. **Conclusion:** That person is guaranteed to be a criminal ($C$ is true).

This conclusion ignores the possibility that the individual **might be a surgeon.** Surgeons perform incisions, which is the act of cutting human flesh in order to perform precise medical procedures on patients. Positing that the surgeon is a criminal is absurd - a **Non-Sequitur.**
